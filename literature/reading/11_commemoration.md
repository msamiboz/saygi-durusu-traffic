### uzun2019 - Hakan Uzun (2019), 10 Kasım ceremonies from the first mourning (1939) to Atatürk Haftası and commemoration without mourning (1988)

Çağdaş Türkiye Tarihi Araştırmaları Dergisi XIX/38 (Spring 2019), pp. 135-170. In Turkish.

- **Question and setting.** How the official and popular commemoration of Atatürk on 10 November changed between the first anniversary (1939) and the abolition of official mourning (1988), and which practices stayed constant (p. 4).

- **Data and method.** Descriptive archival history. Sources: national newspapers (Cumhuriyet, Ulus, Akşam, Milliyet, Hürriyet, Sabah, Zafer and others), the state archives (CDAB; CHP and Halkevi files), parliamentary and MBK minutes, the Official Gazette and the Education Ministry bulletin, a little local press, and two MA theses (Şimşek 2007 for 1938-50; Tatar Hotancı 2017 for the DP years) (pp. 4, 30-33). 1939 is treated in detail, later years in outline (p. 4). No quantitative data except one 1988 newspaper poll. Coverage ends in 1988; the present day appears only in passing remarks.

- **Main findings: dated timeline of the 09:05 practice.**

| Date | What Uzun reports | Page |
|---|---|---|
| 16 Nov 1938 | Government decision for the funeral period (Official Gazette no. 4064): official offices and schools to be closed on 21 Nov 1938; private establishments to be *asked* by the provinces to close; flags at half-mast; entertainment venues not to open; officials to avoid evening parties for a month. Uzun notes that parts of this decree reappear in the 1939 programme. | p. 5 (fn 11) |
| 5 Nov 1939 | The first annual programme, prepared by the CHP General Centre, is made public; the Interior Ministry press directorate sends it to newspapers with a request to comply. Halkevi and CHP provincial and district heads are responsible for carrying it out. | p. 5 |
| 10 Nov 1939 (Friday): programme text | (I) Halkevleri, or party offices where there is none: meeting at 09:05; a designated person recalls that Atatürk died at that hour and invites those present to **five minutes of standing silence**; then a speech and İnönü's declaration; then a wreath at the local statue. (II) Schools: pupils assemble at 09:05, same five-minute standing silence, short talk by a teacher. (III) Press: front pages in a black frame. (IV) Radio: news only, then goes silent; the morning broadcast is arranged so the commemoration falls at 09:05. **The programme covers indoor meetings, schools, press and radio only. It contains nothing on sirens, horns, streets, shops or vehicles.** | p. 6; full text in Appendix 1, pp. 34-35 |
| 10 Nov 1939: character of the programme | Uzun judges that it fixed form and content in detail and left individuals almost no initiative; it was built mainly around mourning. | p. 7 |
| 10 Nov 1939: Ankara | Ceremony begins at 09:05 at the temporary tomb (Ethnography Museum) with a five-minute silence in İnönü's presence. Cinemas closed, flags at half-mast across the country. | p. 7 |
| 10 Nov 1939: Istanbul | Large ceremony at Taksim. During the silence many people kept their shops closed for hours; **trams and vehicles stood where they were for five minutes at 09:05**; boats in the harbour lowered flags to half-mast. Single source: Cumhuriyet, 11 Nov 1939. No sirens or horns are mentioned for 1939. Schools and the university also held the five-minute silence. | p. 8 |
| 1940-1947 | Programme changes are marginal: tomb open to visitors (1940); ceremonies to be extended to villages (1944); other parties' representatives invited (1946); from 1947 the CHP leaves non-party matters to the government. Uzun: the 1939 programme was largely applied in later years, so the ritual became repetitive. | p. 13 |
| 1939-1950 | Every Halkevi sent the CHP General Secretariat a report on how its ceremony was held (speeches and poems attached). | p. 14 |
| 10 Nov 1947 | Parliament observes a five-minute standing silence and adjourns. | pp. 13-14 |
| 1950-1960 (DP period) | Entertainment venues and theatres closed, flags at half-mast, **cars sounded their horns and factories and ferries their sirens**. This is the first place in the article where horns and sirens appear, and it refers to the DP period (source: Tatar thesis pp. 58-107). The government stops being the sole organiser; events organised by the public without government direction increase; student organisations become active. | p. 15 |
| 1950 onward | Opposition parties hold alternative ceremonies; the day becomes a standing subject of party polemic. From 1956 governors refuse the CHP permission to hold ceremonies. | pp. 16-17 |
| 10 Nov 1951 | President Bayar visits the temporary tomb at exactly 09:05; PM Menderes arrives about 09:30 and is attacked in Ulus for breaking a tradition kept since 1939. Egyptian consulate criticised for not flying its flag. | p. 17 (fn 74), p. 18 |
| 10 Nov 1953 | Burial at Anıtkabir; it becomes the centre of the official ceremony from then on. Reading İnönü's declaration at every ceremony ends. | p. 15 |
| 10 Nov 1959 | A Greek freighter that did not lower its flag and did not join the sirens is confronted by people gathered on the quay until the flag is lowered. | p. 18 |
| 8-10 Nov 1960 | After the 27 May coup, MBK member Muzaffer Özdağ announces an *Atatürk Haftası* starting 10 November and says the day should not be a day of mourning. Criticised inside the MBK, he clarifies that this is not a change of law: papers will still appear with black headlines and flags will still be lowered, following the official procedure. (His MBK statement gives 10-17 November; the later standard is 10-16 November.) | pp. 18-20 (fn 86, 89) |
| 1960-1988 | The day is spread over a week of talks and cultural events, but on 10 November itself papers still carry black headlines, **at 09:05 a silence is observed across the country, car horns sound together with the sirens of ferries, ships and factories**, and flags are at half-mast (Cumhuriyet and Milliyet, 10-11 Nov 1960). | pp. 21-22 |
| 7 Sep 1963 | Education Ministry board decision no. 2016-10 makes 10-16 Nov 1963 *Atatürk Haftası* in schools (25th anniversary); this framework governs school programmes until 1988. | p. 21 |
| 1968 | With regular TV broadcasting (from 31 Jan 1968), special programmes on 10 November. | p. 22 |
| 1952, 1972 | Calls to end mourning: Education Minister Tevfik İleri (1952); Nadir Nadi's 1972 column lists what the mourning consisted of: half-mast flags, black headlines, closed theatres and entertainment venues, an alcohol ban. | p. 23 |
| 1987 | The governing ANAP puts abolition of mourning on the agenda. | p. 24 |
| 8 Oct 1988 | Tourism Minister Tınaz Titiz: on the 50th anniversary Atatürk will be commemorated without mourning; the prohibitions applied on 10 November in earlier years are lifted; the flow of daily life will not change. The Ministry of Culture and Tourism is put in charge. | p. 24 |
| 10 Nov 1988 | Özal: first commemoration without mourning; a week around 10 November is to be devoted to commemoration. On the day, the 09:05 silence is again observed, horns and the sirens of ferries, ships and factories sound, flags are at half-mast; the difference from earlier years is that entertainment venues stay open and papers do not use black headlines. | pp. 24, 26-27 |

- **The 1988 PİAR poll for Sabah (published 10 Nov 1988), exact figures (p. 25, fn 103):**
  - Had heard of the decision to commemorate without mourning: 49.1%; had not: 50.9%.
  - Approve lifting the mourning: 74.5%; disapprove: 25.4%.
  - Want international cinema, theatre and music festivals and sports events in Atatürk's memory on 10 November: 80%; do not: 20%.
  - Believe a standing silence should be held at 09:05, the hour of death: **89.7%**; not necessary: **7.8%**; no opinion: **2.5%**.
  - Uzun gives no sample size, sampling frame, mode or coverage. He also reports that those who wanted mourning kept still opposed closing restaurants, cinemas and theatres (p. 25).

- **Legal obligation versus voluntary practice (what Uzun actually says).**
  - Abstract: some behaviours performed during the mourning regime were adopted and continued by a wide section of society "herhangi bir zorunluluk olmadığı halde" (p. 1), and became tradition.
  - After 1988 the silence, horns, sirens and half-mast flags did not persist because people still wanted to mourn; they continue because the public wants to keep a tradition (p. 27).
  - Conclusion: people who never saw Atatürk show gratitude on 10 November by their own will and without direction from anywhere (p. 28). Attitudes on the day have become a yardstick, at least for some groups, of attachment to Atatürk (p. 28). Participation in ceremonies has risen over the years, depending also on the political atmosphere (p. 28).
  - The constant practices he lists: silence at 09:05; horns plus ship and factory sirens, with life in the country almost coming to a complete stop at that moment; half-mast flags on all official buildings and missions abroad; official ceremony at Anıtkabir since 1953 (pp. 27-28).
  - What was regulated: the 1938 decree (closures, flags; private firms only requested); the 1939 CHP programme for party houses, schools, press and radio; an official procedure for black headlines and half-mast flags still in force in 1960 (p. 20); prohibitions (venue closures, alcohol) in force until lifted in 1988 (pp. 23-24). **Nowhere does Uzun cite a rule that obliges drivers or pedestrians to stop, and nowhere does he cite a rule ordering sirens.**

- **Usable for our paper.**
  - Introduction: the 09:05 stop of vehicles is as old as the ritual; Istanbul trams and vehicles stood still for five minutes on the first anniversary (p. 8). A historian describes life as almost stopping at that moment (p. 27); we measure how much.
  - Introduction / institutional background: the sound signal described is car horns plus sirens of factories, ferries and ships (pp. 3, 15, 21, 26); the opening page describes the present-day siren with accompanying horns as a call to stand in silence, even if only for one minute (p. 3).
  - Literature: the practice survived the end of official mourning in 1988 unchanged (pp. 26-27), and the stated norm was near-consensual in 1988 (89.7%, p. 25).
  - Results-Discussion: the stop is described as non-obligatory and self-sustained (pp. 1, 27, 28), which supports reading the slowdown as norm-driven behaviour. Participation is said to move with the political climate (p. 28), which motivates looking at year-by-year estimates.
  - Data / Method: schools and public institutions hold a ceremony at 09:05 by long-standing programme (pp. 6, 21), so the morning travel pattern on 10 November may differ from control days for reasons other than the stop itself.

- **Caveats.**
  - The tram and vehicle stop rests on one newspaper report for one city in 1939. Uzun gives no evidence on vehicles for any later year.
  - **Correction to `literature/notes/commemoration_turkey.md`:** the horns-and-sirens sentence on printed p. 149 refers to the DP period (1950s), not the 1940s. Uzun never states when sirens began, and footnote 6 (which the earlier note hoped would date it) only cites Ökten on remembered closures. The earliest siren evidence in Uzun is therefore the 1950s, with a concrete incident in 1959.
  - The sirens Uzun describes belong to factories, ferries and ships. He says nothing about municipal or civil-defence sirens, who triggers them today, or since when.
  - Duration: five minutes in 1939 and 1947; Uzun's only present-day remark is one minute (p. 3). He does not say when or how it shortened. The two-minute figure in our earlier notes comes from news sites, not from Uzun.
  - The no-obligation statement is a historian's assertion, not a legal analysis. It is consistent with, but does not prove, the absence of a regulation.
  - The poll measures agreement that the silence *should* be held, not behaviour, and not drivers. Method unknown.
  - Page fixes for the earlier note: Istanbul trams = printed p. 142; Greek freighter = p. 152; 1988 day description = pp. 160-161.

- **Checks or analyses it suggests for us.**
  - Report the estimate next to the nominal duration T of the silence. If a share s of vehicles on the road stops for T minutes, then full-stop-equivalent minutes m is roughly s*T, so s is roughly m/T: with m = 0.73, s is about 0.73 for T = 1 and about 0.37 for T = 2 **[our inference]**. State clearly that this is rough (queue discharge pushes it up, exclusion of stopped vehicles pushes it down) and that T must be documented from a primary source.
  - Document the present-day siren (who sounds it, exact start and length) from a primary source; Uzun cannot be cited for this.
  - Because schools and offices hold ceremonies at 09:05, test whether the 08h and 10h reference hours are themselves unusual on 10 November (placebo contrasts using 07h and 11h, or each neighbour hour separately).
  - Official ceremonies and crowds (Taksim in Uzun, p. 8) may involve road closures; drop cells near ceremony sites as a robustness check.
  - 10 November 2024 was a Sunday (2020 Tue, 2021 Wed, 2022 Thu, 2023 Fri). Show the estimate with and without 2024.

### ozyurek2004 - Esra Özyürek (2004), Miniaturizing Atatürk: privatization of state imagery and ideology in Turkey

American Ethnologist 31(3): 374-391.

- **Question and setting.** Why, from the late 1990s, Turkish consumers began buying Atatürk pictures and objects for homes, shops and their own bodies, and what this says about how citizens relate to the state under neoliberal and Islamist challenges (pp. 1-2).

- **Data and method.** Ethnography in Istanbul in the late 1990s: conversations and interviews with shop owners, a street peddler, a statue producer, a photo-studio owner, activists and friends; observation at the 1998 exhibitions and at a film screening; reading of newspapers, ads, a state film and books. The number of interviews and the sampling are not reported. No quantitative data. Interpretive argument.

- **Main findings.**
  - What is new in the 1990s is the privatisation of the production, circulation and consumption of Atatürk's image, and its content: small, smiling, full-body, social images instead of large, stern, official busts (pp. 2, 7-9, 12).
  - Trigger: the public visibility of Islamic symbols and the Welfare Party's 1994-95 election wins. Kemalists answered with a commodified counter-symbolism; the veil and the Atatürk portrait became competing identity markers (pp. 4-5).
  - For the first time citizens saw the official ideology as needing their personal protection and took personal responsibility for promoting it (p. 5).
  - Forced versus voluntary: informants contrast the top-down Atatürk campaign of the 1980-83 junta, which they say alienated people, with the late-1990s interest, which they describe as freely chosen. Buying the image in the market is taken as the proof of voluntariness (pp. 6-7). The trend peaked after the February 1997 military intervention (p. 6).
  - The 75th anniversary celebrations in 1998 were organised by civil-society organisations, not the military, and stressed spontaneity (p. 6).
  - Paradox in the conclusion: presenting support as voluntary masks state power, and carries state symbolism into private spheres it had not reached before (pp. 4, 15).
  - **Who participates.** Buyers were mostly middle and upper-middle class, Turkish (not Kurdish), secular urbanites settled in a big city for two generations, not at either political extreme, and firmly against the Islamist movement (p. 2). In the 1999 election most said they voted for the Democratic Left Party (29% nationally) or the CHP (9%), a few for the Motherland Party (15%) (p. 15, note 1). Examples come from Erenköy, described as an upscale, mostly secular neighbourhood, and from Beyoğlu (p. 5). The military put up new statues in shantytown neighbourhoods that had voted Islamist in 1995 (p. 5). Critics of Kemalism named: Islamists, Kurdish nationalists, liberal intellectuals (pp. 4, 6). An Alevi village is described as traditionally devoted to Atatürk (p. 8; p. 15 note 11). Islamists also began to re-appropriate Atatürk imagery (p. 15, note 4).

- **Usable for our paper.**
  - Literature: the shift from imposed to self-declared voluntary Kemalist practice in the 1990s (pp. 6-7); the idea that citizens now personally uphold a state ritual (p. 5).
  - Results-Discussion (heterogeneity): the social profile on p. 2 is the only explicit statement in our reading set of who the voluntary participants are. It predicts stronger participation in established, higher-income, secular districts and weaker participation in peripheral, more religious districts **[our inference; the paper is about buying images]**.
  - Results-Discussion: the masking argument (pp. 4, 15) is a caution against describing the stop as purely voluntary.

- **Caveats.**
  - The article does not discuss the 09:05 silence, sirens, 10 November ceremonies or traffic. 10 November appears only as the 60th-anniversary newspaper photo spreads in 1998 (p. 14) and in a citation of Ökten 2001.
  - Fieldwork is 20 to 25 years older than our data, with a non-random set of Istanbul informants.
  - **Correction to earlier notes:** the note that this literature shows 10 Kasım participation being embraced more by CHP-leaning citizens overstates this paper. It supports secular, middle-class, anti-Islamist; the informants mostly voted DSP, not CHP.
  - Voluntariness is the informants' self-description, which Özyürek herself treats critically.

- **Checks or analyses it suggests for us.**
  - One pre-specified heterogeneity test on a secular-versus-religious proxy at district level (for example vote shares, education, income), within speed class so that road type does not drive it.
  - Do not use CHP share alone; the supported dimension is secular versus Islamist, and support crossed party lines.
  - Vehicles on an arterial road are mostly not residents of that district, so district composition is a noisy proxy; expect attenuation and say so **[our inference]**.

### goldman2018 - Anat Goldman (2018), Privatized commemoration, political polarization, and the cult of Atatürk since the mid-1990s

The Journal of the Middle East and Africa, online first 24 Aug 2018, DOI 10.1080/21520844.2018.1485347 (the PDF carries no volume or issue).

- **Question and setting.** Why the Atatürk cult persists and even widens under a governing party that does not fully want it, and how it became both a state cult and a fragmented popular one (pp. 2, 4).

- **Data and method.** Interpretive essay built on secondary literature (Özyürek, Navaro-Yashin, Ozkan and Foster, Öktem), press and social-media items, and newspaper advertisements. No systematic data collection, no sample, no measure of participation. The author's dissertation compares Israeli Memorial Day and Atatürk Memorial Day (p. 17).

- **Main findings.**
  - The 9:05 standing on 10 November is described as a repertoire of public engagement known to all Turks; the 2013 Standing Man protest in Taksim borrowed it and inverted its meaning (pp. 2-4). She states that the minute of silence originally symbolises individual compliance, the merging of one's will and time with the state's (p. 4); no source is given for this.
  - Argument: the state pulled back from pushing Kemalism in public from 1983; neoliberal reform and consumer culture let private actors take over; commodification and personal appropriation blurred the lines between state and society and between ideologies; the cult became more inclusive and now engages more people than before (pp. 5, 15-17).
  - Current practice: between 29 October and 10 November and in the following Atatürk Week, individuals, firms and state bodies decorate homes, cars and workplaces; on 10 November many people wear national colours or Atatürk T-shirts for the 9:05 silence; in Ankara many visit Anıtkabir, in Istanbul many go to Dolmabahçe Palace (pp. 11-12).
  - Corporations took over a formerly state role: first 10 November newspaper ads in 2004; from 2005-06 full-page ads by large firms; Koç Holding took the back page of Hürriyet every 10 November from 2008 (pp. 13-14).
  - Opposition meaning: on 10 Nov 2013, five months after Gezi, a record 1,089,615 people visited Anıtkabir in one day, more than double the previous year; readers of Radikal gave reasons such as resistance to oppression, some saying explicitly it was not out of love for Atatürk (pp. 14-15).
  - Blurring after 15 July 2016: a large Atatürk portrait on the AKP building; Erdoğan's 7 Aug 2016 rally and his 10 Nov 2017 speech invoked Atatürk; it has become hard to separate secularist from conservative participation, or state from society (pp. 15-16).
  - She criticises Özyürek for drawing too sharp a Kemalist/Islamist line and for ignoring generational conflicts among secularists (p. 12).
  - Background relevant to geography: Islamist networks built their base by providing services on the growing edges of big cities after the 1980s (p. 10).

- **Usable for our paper.**
  - Introduction: the 9:05 standing as a universally known national repertoire (p. 4).
  - Literature: privatised, voluntary commemoration that is partly directed against the government (pp. 4, 15); the 2013 Anıtkabir record as evidence that participation responds to the political climate (p. 14).
  - Results-Discussion (heterogeneity): two opposing predictions. (a) Participation as an opposition or secular identity act implies a gradient by district politics. (b) Broadened, cross-camp participation after 2016 implies a flat gradient in 2020-2024. A small or null gradient is therefore informative, not a failure.
  - Data / Robustness: many people travel to Dolmabahçe on 10 November in Istanbul (p. 12), a local traffic shock unrelated to the stop.

- **Caveats.**
  - No evidence on how many people observe the silence, or where. The only number is the Anıtkabir visitor count for Ankara.
  - The claim that the state has given up the commemorative role (p. 5) sits uneasily with continuing official ceremonies and with her own account of Erdoğan's 2017 speech (p. 16); treat it as her interpretation.
  - Sources are largely journalistic; the origin story of the minute of silence (p. 4) is unsourced.
  - **Correction to earlier notes:** Goldman does not predict stronger participation in CHP districts. Her thesis is that the lines blurred. The earlier note's volume and issue (9(2)) cannot be confirmed from the PDF.

- **Checks or analyses it suggests for us.**
  - Report year-by-year estimates descriptively (five points, no inference) to see whether the effect moves with the political calendar.
  - Exclude cells around Dolmabahçe, Beşiktaş and Taksim as a robustness check for ceremony traffic.
  - If a district gradient is tested, pre-specify the sign from (a) and discuss (b) as the alternative before looking.

### karakus2023 - Gülbeyaz Karakuş (2023), A political-theological issue: is Kemalism a civil religion or a political religion?

Journal of Economy Culture and Society 68: 111-118, DOI 10.26650/JECS2023-1258297.

- **Question and setting.** Whether early Kemalism (defined as 1920-1938, p. 4 note 1) achieved secularisation or built its own civil or political religion, and whether the two labels can be told apart (p. 2).

- **Data and method.** Short conceptual essay. Draws on Rousseau, Schmitt, Gauchet, Gentile and Atalay (2018), illustrated with a few period texts: a 1923 parliamentary speech, Akseki's 1925 religious manual for soldiers, a 1933 newspaper passage, the Nutuk. No empirical design.

- **Main findings.**
  - Concepts: civil religion comes from Rousseau's religion of the citizen and Durkheim's solidarity-producing religion; Bellah is named once as the author who applied it to the United States (p. 2). Following Gentile, political religion is the sacralisation of politics by totalitarian, exclusive regimes, while civil religion sacralises collective life without a single political movement and tolerates other beliefs; Gentile admits the boundary is not sharp and that one can turn into the other (pp. 2-3).
  - Thesis: for Kemalism the two cannot be separated categorically. The Republic sacralised nation, homeland, national sovereignty, parliament, regime and leader, and did so partly by borrowing from Islam instead of breaking with it (pp. 4-7).
  - Evidence offered: sovereignty moved from God and sultan to the nation, and parliament was spoken of in sacred terms (pp. 4-5); military service and obedience to the state were taught as religious duties in Akseki's manual (p. 6); the leader was given quasi-divine attributes (p. 6).
  - Ritual: holidays and rituals, and Atatürk statues and busts placed across the country, reminded citizens of loyalty and civic debt and produced a sense of homogeneity; inclusive ceremonies also conceal difference and secure one community's dominance over others (p. 6).
  - Atalay (2018) classes interwar Kemalism as a political religion comparable to interwar totalitarian regimes; Karakuş replies that a top-down civil religion in Rousseau's sense is not different in kind (p. 5).

- **Usable for our paper.**
  - Literature: one sentence that Turkish scholarship debates whether Kemalism is a civil or a political religion and that the distinction is contested (pp. 2-3, 7); the function of ritual and statues in producing loyalty (p. 6).
  - Introduction: frames the 09:05 stop as a ritual of a secular sacred **[our inference; the article never mentions it]**.

- **Caveats.**
  - The article ends in 1938. It does not mention 10 November, the silence, sirens, or any commemoration of Atatürk's death, and says nothing about who participates.
  - **Correction to earlier notes:** it does not apply Bellah. Bellah gets one sentence and a reference entry (to a 1988 Daedalus reprint). The framework is Rousseau and Gentile. Author first name and DOI are now confirmed from the PDF; Atalay's first name is Onur (p. 5).
  - Thin evidence, assertive style, some garbled sentences. The back matter declares ethics approval from a Norwegian data centre and informed consent from participants, although the article has no participants (p. 7), which suggests weak editorial control. Cite for the existence of the debate, not as authority.

- **Checks or analyses it suggests for us.** None for the empirical design. For framing, use the neutral term civil religion with a note that the label is contested, and avoid claims about sincerity of belief.

### vinitzky2010 - Vered Vinitzky-Seroussi and Chana Teeger (2010), Unpacking the unspoken: silence in collective memory and forgetting

Social Forces 88(3): 1103-1122.

- **Question and setting.** How silence, usually equated with forgetting, can serve either memory or forgetting; illustrated with the commemoration of Yitzhak Rabin's assassination in Israel (pp. 1-3).

- **Data and method.** Qualitative, collected over about ten years by the first author: observation at coerced commemorations (state schools, official graveside ceremony) and elective ones (annual rally in Rabin Square); 24 in-depth interviews with agents of memory; observation in 30 Jewish state and state-religious schools on Rabin Memorial Day in 1997 and 2005; content analysis of all articles on Rabin in seven Israeli newspapers in three languages around the memorial day in 1996 and 2000 (p. 4; p. 17 note 3). No quantitative estimates.

- **Main findings.**
  - Typology (Table 1, p. 6): type of silence (overt = literal absence of speech; covert = absence of content hidden inside much talk) crossed with aim (memory or forgetting).

    | | Overt | Covert |
    |---|---|---|
    | Memory | moment of silence | *bland commemoration* |
    | Forgetting | pervasive silence | *cacophonous commemoration* |

  - The moment of silence (overt, memory) is intentional, bounded, planned and framed by commemoration. It interrupts the flow of time, movement, speech and thought, and often recurs at the exact time of the commemorated event. The authors consider it especially powerful when wrapped in a nationwide siren and a norm to stand still, and call the annual, official version arguably the strongest expression of a wish to remember (pp. 6-7), and perhaps the highest official honour to the past (p. 15).
  - Israeli sirens (p. 7): Holocaust Memorial Day, one siren at 10:00 lasting one minute. Memorial Day for the Fallen Soldiers, a one-minute siren the evening before and a two-minute siren at 11:00 that opens the ceremonies. The norm is to stop whatever one is doing and stand for the duration. The sirens use the state's wartime warning infrastructure (p. 17 note 5).
  - **Traffic:** drivers on the highway are commonly seen stopping their cars, stepping out and standing until the siren ends (p. 7).
  - Internalisation: children are socialised early; people stand even when alone at home or in the office. Drawing on Durkheim and Foucault, the authors read this as social control internalised without external surveillance (p. 7).
  - Hierarchy: Rabin Memorial Day has a moment of silence at ceremonies but no national siren; which days receive a siren ranks their national importance (p. 7).
  - Non-participation: ideological state-religious schools in settlements held no ceremony in 2005 despite the 1997 law; ultra-Orthodox schools are assumed to ignore all state memorial days; Arabic-language press and Palestinian schools fell silent after initial mourning (pp. 8-9).
  - Covert types: Rabin's agents of memory dropped the political context of the assassination to widen the audience (pp. 9-12); state-religious schools diluted the day by adding Rachel and a prayer for rain (pp. 12-14).
  - Conflict: overt silences are more conflict-prone than covert ones; in the memory domain, people who break the norm of the moment of silence provoke antagonism (p. 15). Moments of silence can lose distinctiveness as they multiply (p. 16).

- **Usable for our paper.**
  - Introduction / Literature: the closest conceptual statement of what the 09:05 stop is, namely a siren-framed moment of silence repeated at the exact time of the event (pp. 6-7); Israel as the direct comparator, including cars stopping on highways (p. 7).
  - Literature: compliance is normative and internalised, not policed (p. 7), which matches Uzun's no-obligation description.
  - Results-Discussion: groups estranged from the state opt out visibly (pp. 8-9), a mechanism for spatial heterogeneity; social sanction of non-compliers (p. 15) as the enforcement channel.
  - Results-Discussion: covert silences in Turkey could be discussed with Uzun's Atatürk Week and the 1988 reform **[our inference]**.

- **Caveats.**
  - Nothing on Turkey. Nothing quantitative: the highway observation is a sentence of ethnographic description, with no count, share, or measure of traffic.
  - The moment of silence takes about one page of the article; the empirical core concerns Rabin, a contested figure and a day without a siren.
  - The Israeli sirens are state-operated and backed by memorial-day laws; the paper does not say whether stopping is legally required. Do not cite it for the legal status of stopping in either country.
  - The docile-bodies reading is interpretation, not a tested claim.

- **Checks or analyses it suggests for us.**
  - Cite the Israeli durations (one and two minutes) as benchmarks next to our full-stop-equivalent minutes.
  - On a road, one stopped vehicle blocks those behind it, so road compliance is partly mechanical; describe the estimate as collective compliance, not as a share of willing individuals **[our inference]**.
  - A neighbourhood-level opt-out, as in the Israeli schools, would appear as near-zero effects in particular districts; a map of cell-level contrasts is a cheap descriptive check, without inference.
  - Note that no study in this set measures compliance with a siren; our paper appears to be the first to quantify it.

### bellah1967 - Robert N. Bellah (1967; reprinted 2005), Civil religion in America

Original: Daedalus 96(1), Winter 1967. The PDF is the reprint in Daedalus, Fall 2005, pp. 40-55; page numbers here refer to the reprint.

- **Question and setting.** Whether the United States has, alongside the churches and distinct from them, an institutionalised civil religion, and what its content and risks are (p. 1).

- **Data and method.** Interpretive essay. Close reading of presidential inaugural addresses (Kennedy, Washington, Jefferson, Lincoln, Johnson), founding documents, and national holidays and sites, with Rousseau, Tocqueville, Durkheim and Lloyd Warner as references. No empirical design.

- **Main findings.**
  - Definition: the separation of church and state did not remove a religious dimension from politics. Americans share common elements of religious orientation, expressed in beliefs, symbols and rituals that Bellah names the American civil religion (p. 3); later, a collection of such beliefs, symbols and rituals about sacred things, institutionalised in a collectivity (p. 7). The term is Rousseau's (p. 4). A footnote invokes the Durkheimian idea that every group has a religious dimension (p. 1 note 1).
  - Ritual should not be dismissed as mere ceremony; what is said on solemn occasions points to deep values not voiced in everyday life (p. 2).
  - Themes: God as a non-sectarian central symbol; America as a new Israel (pp. 6-7); after the Civil War, death, sacrifice and rebirth, embodied in Lincoln as the martyred president (pp. 8-9).
  - Ritual calendar and sacred places: Memorial Day ties the local community to the national cult, Thanksgiving ties the family to it; with the Fourth of July, Veterans Day and the two presidential birthdays they form an annual calendar, and public schools are a main setting for the rites (p. 10). Arlington and Gettysburg are the hallowed sites (p. 9).
  - The American version was never anticlerical or militantly secular and borrowed from the churches without conflict, which let it mobilise deep personal motivation for national goals (p. 11). **France is the contrast:** the Revolution was anticlerical and tried to found an anti-Christian civil religion, leaving a lasting gulf between Catholic and revolutionary symbols (p. 12).
  - Civil religion can be distorted and used to cover narrow interests; it needs a standard above the nation (pp. 12-16). It can change when its central symbols are questioned (p. 13).

- **How it applies to Kemalism.**
  - Bellah does not mention Turkey or Atatürk. Any application is by analogy **[our inference]**.
  - Elements that map: a founder treated as a sacred figure (compare Lincoln); a fixed annual calendar; a national mausoleum (Anıtkabir, as in Uzun p. 15); schools as the main ritual setting (Uzun pp. 6, 21); a synchronised act at the hour of death.
  - Elements that do not map: Bellah's civil religion has God at its centre and coexists smoothly with the churches. Kemalism is laicist and was built in tension with established religion, so in Bellah's own terms it resembles the French case (p. 12) more than the American one. Karakuş argues it nonetheless borrowed heavily from Islam (karakus2023 pp. 5-7).
  - Bellah's civil religion is not state-decreed in origin; the 1939 programme was a single-party directive (Uzun pp. 5-7). This is why some authors prefer political religion for the early period (karakus2023 p. 5) and why the post-1988 voluntary persistence (Uzun p. 27) is the part that fits civil religion best.

- **Usable for our paper.**
  - Introduction / Literature: one sentence defining civil religion and its ritual calendar (pp. 3, 10), with Memorial Day as the example of a rite binding localities to the nation.
  - Literature: the warning against dismissing ritual as empty (p. 2) supports treating a one-minute stop as meaningful behaviour.
  - Results-Discussion: the France contrast (p. 12) to qualify the analogy.

- **Caveats.**
  - No evidence on participation, compliance or heterogeneity; nothing on moments of silence or sirens.
  - United States, 1960s, written against the Vietnam War; much of the essay is normative.
  - Cite as the origin of the concept only. The reprint's volume number is not printed in the PDF text; cite the 1967 original (96(1)) as stated on p. 1 of the reprint and check its page range before using it.

- **Checks or analyses it suggests for us.** None for the design. In writing, keep the civil-religion frame to a sentence or two and attribute the Kemalism application to the Turkish literature, with the caveat above.

## Cross-paper synthesis

1. The vehicle stop is as old as the ritual (Istanbul, 10 Nov 1939, five minutes; Uzun p. 8), but it was never in the 1939 programme, which covered only party houses, schools, press and radio (Uzun pp. 34-35).
2. Horns and sirens are first reported for the 1950s, and they are factory, ferry and ship sirens (Uzun pp. 15, 18, 21); no source here dates or documents today's siren, so the paper needs a primary source for who sounds it and for how long.
3. Official mourning ended in 1988, yet the silence, horns and sirens continued that same year (Uzun pp. 26-27); 89.7% said the 09:05 silence should be held (p. 25), a stated norm, not measured behaviour.
4. No paper in this group cites a legal duty for drivers to stop; Uzun calls the practice non-obligatory (p. 1), and Vinitzky-Seroussi and Teeger describe the Israeli equivalent as internalised without surveillance (p. 7). Call our outcome norm-driven compliance, and keep the legal question open.
5. Vinitzky-Seroussi and Teeger give the concept (overt silence in the service of memory, framed by a siren at the exact hour) and the comparator (Israeli one- and two-minute sirens, cars stopping on highways, p. 7); none of the six papers measures compliance, so quantifying it is new.
6. Who participates: Özyürek's 1990s voluntary Kemalists were secular, middle-class, long-settled urban and anti-Islamist (p. 2), which predicts a district gradient; Goldman argues the lines blurred after 2013-2016 (pp. 15-16), which predicts a flat one in 2020-2024.
7. So test one pre-specified secular-versus-religious district interaction within speed class, expect attenuation from through-traffic and mechanical blocking, and treat a null as consistent with Goldman.
8. Participation is said to track the political climate (Uzun p. 28; Goldman p. 14: Anıtkabir visits doubled in 2013); show year-by-year estimates descriptively.
9. Design threats from the ritual itself: 09:05 ceremonies in schools and offices may shift the 08h and 10h baselines, and crowds at Dolmabahçe and Taksim are local shocks (Uzun pp. 6, 8; Goldman p. 12); 10 Nov 2024 was a Sunday.
10. Civil religion (Bellah pp. 3, 10) is a usable one-sentence frame, but Kemalism fits Bellah's French contrast better than his American case (p. 12) and Karakuş shows the label is contested (p. 7); neither says anything about 10 November.
